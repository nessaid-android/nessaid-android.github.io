The rules of the thirteen games

Each game also has its rules in the app, under How to play, in every language the app has.

Brisca

Three cards, a trump turned up, and the aces and threes to take. Two or four players.

Brisca is played one against one, or two against two with partners facing, with the Spanish pack of forty: ace to seven, and the sota, caballo and rey of oros, copas, espadas and bastos.

Each player is dealt three cards and the next card is turned up under the stock: its suit is trumps for the hand. The player after the dealer leads, and play goes round anticlockwise unless the table chose otherwise.

Nobody has to follow suit or to beat the card led. The highest trump takes the trick; with no trump in it, the highest card of the suit led. From highest to lowest a suit runs: ace, three, rey, caballo, sota, 7, 6, 5, 4, 2. After each trick its winner draws from the stock, then the others in turn, so every hand is back to three; the turned card is the last to be drawn. Then the last cards are played out.

The cards count: ace 11, three 10, rey 4, caballo 3, sota 2, the rest nothing - 120 in all. More than 60 takes the hand; 60 each is a draw. The first side to take the number of hands the table chose wins the game.

On your turn, while there is a stock, the seven of trumps can be changed for a turned ace, three or figure, and the two of trumps for a turned four to seven. The host can turn this off.

Bisca and Briscola

The table can play Brisca's two cousins instead.

Bisca, as Portugal plays it: the seven is second only to the ace and counts ten, and the three is a plain card worth nothing. From highest to lowest a suit runs: ace, 7, rey, sota, caballo, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2; the sota counts 3 and the caballo 2. The two of trumps may be changed for the turned card, on your turn while there is a stock.

Briscola, as Italy plays it: Brisca's ranking and count, the three second to the ace, and no card is ever changed for the turned one. Everything else is the same: three cards, a trump turned up, a draw after every trick, and more than 60 of the 120 points to take the hand.

Tute

Follow suit, sing the twenty and the forty, and take the last trick. Four in pairs, or two.

Tute is played two against two with partners facing, or one against one, with the Spanish pack of forty: ace to seven, and the sota, caballo and rey of oros, copas, espadas and bastos.

At four, every card is dealt, ten each, and the dealer's last card is shown: its suit is trumps, and the card stays in the dealer's hand. At two, each is dealt eight and the next card is turned up under the stock for trumps; after each trick its winner draws, then the other.

The player after the dealer leads, and play goes round anticlockwise unless the table chose otherwise. At four, and at two once the stock is gone, play is de arrastre: follow the suit led, and beat the best card of it if you can; with none of it, play a trump, higher than any trump already in the trick if you can; with no trump that has to be played, play anything. While there is a stock at two, play anything.

The highest trump takes the trick; with no trump in it, the highest card of the suit led. From highest to lowest a suit runs: ace, three, rey, caballo, sota, 7, 6, 5, 4, 2.

The cards count: ace 11, three 10, rey 4, caballo 3, sota 2, the rest nothing; the last trick adds 10, so 130 in all. On your lead straight after taking a trick you may sing, once: a rey and caballo of one suit held together count 20, or 40 in trumps. All four reyes, or all four caballos, are a tute, and take the hand at once.

The side with more points takes the hand. The first side to take the number of hands the table chose wins the game. At two, on your turn while there is a stock, the seven of trumps can be changed for a turned ace, three or figure, and the two for a turned four to seven; the host can turn this off.

Tute cabrón and Tute subastado

The table can play one of two other ways of Tute instead.

Tute cabrón is for three, each playing alone. The two of bastos is left out, so each has thirteen cards; the dealer's last card is shown for trumps, and play is bound from the first card, as at four. Whoever takes a trick may sing, as always. The hand goes to the player with most points; with the most level, nobody takes it.

Tute subastado is for four in two pairs. Every card is dealt and none is shown: there are no trumps until the bidding is over. Starting with the player after the dealer, each bids for the right to name trumps, from 70 and ten higher each time, or passes and is out of the bidding. When everybody else has passed with nobody bidding, the last player has to bid 70. The winner of the bidding names trumps and leads the first trick. Their pair has to make at least what they bid, in cards, the last trick and songs together; then the hand is theirs, and if not it goes to the other pair. A tute takes the hand as always.

Guiñote

Aragón's cousin of Tute: six cards and a stock, the twenty and the forty, and the first pair to 101.

Guiñote is played two against two with partners facing, or one against one, with the Spanish pack of forty: ace to seven, and the sota, caballo and rey of oros, copas, espadas and bastos.

Each player is dealt six cards and the next is turned up under the stock: its suit is trumps for the hand. After each trick its winner draws from the stock, then the others in turn; the turned card is the last to be drawn. The player after the dealer leads, and play goes round anticlockwise unless the table chose otherwise.

While there is a stock, play any card. Once it is gone, play is de arrastre: follow the suit led, and beat the best card of it if you can; with none of it, play a trump, higher than any trump already in the trick if you can; with no trump that has to be played, play anything.

The highest trump takes the trick; with no trump in it, the highest card of the suit led. From highest to lowest a suit runs: ace, three, rey, caballo, sota, 7, 6, 5, 4, 2.

The cards count: ace 11, three 10, rey 4, caballo 3, sota 2, the rest nothing; the last trick adds 10, so 130 in all. On your lead straight after taking a trick you may sing, once: a rey and caballo of one suit held together count 20, or 40 in trumps.

Each side's points carry from hand to hand. The first side past 101 (or the target the table chose) wins the game; if both pass it in the same hand, the higher score wins. On your turn while there is a stock, the seven of trumps can be changed for a turned ace, three or figure, and the two for a turned four to seven; the host can turn this off.

Escoba

Take what makes fifteen with a card from your hand, and sweep the table for an escoba. Two players, or four in pairs.

Escoba de quince is played one against one, or two against two with partners facing, with the Spanish pack of forty. Each card is worth its number; the sota is 8, the caballo 9 and the rey 10.

Each player is dealt three cards and four are laid face up on the table. If those four make 15 (or 30), the dealer takes them as an escoba (or two). The player after the dealer plays first, and play goes round anticlockwise unless the table chose otherwise.

On your turn you play one card from your hand. With it you may take any cards on the table that make 15 together with it: choose your card, tap the table cards, and Take. If it takes nothing, lay it down on the table. Taking every card on the table is an escoba, worth a point.

When everybody's hand is empty the dealer deals three more each, until the pack is gone. The cards left on the table at the end go to whoever took cards last.

Then each side counts: a point for the most cards, a point for the most oros, a point for the seven of oros, a point for the most sevens, and a point for each escoba. Level counts score nothing. The points carry from hand to hand, and the first side to the target the table chose wins the game.

Scopa and Chkobba

The table can play Escoba's two cousins instead. Both take by value rather than by fifteen.

Your card takes a table card of the same value; if there is none, it takes cards adding up to its value. A single card of its value has to be taken before any sum, and a card that can take must take. The sota is 8, the caballo 9 and the rey 10. Taking every card on the table is a sweep, worth a point, but not on the last take of the hand; and the first four on the table are never taken by the dealer.

Scopa, as Italy plays it, counts the most cards, the most oros, the seven of oros, the primiera and the sweeps. The primiera is the best card of each suit added up: a 7 is 21, a 6 18, an ace 16, a 5 15, a 4 14, a 3 13, a 2 12 and a figure 10; without all four suits it counts nothing.

Chkobba, as Tunisia plays it, counts the most cards, the most oros, the seven of oros, the most sevens (the most sixes when the sevens are level) and the sweeps, the chkobbas.

Cinquillo

Build the four suits out from their fives, a card at a time; the first out wins. Two to four players.

Cinquillo is played by two, three or four, each for themselves, with the Spanish pack of forty: ace to seven, and the sota, caballo and rey of oros, copas, espadas and bastos.

Every card is dealt, one at a time; with three, one player has a card more. The player holding the five of oros opens the hand with it. Then play goes round, anticlockwise unless the table chose otherwise.

On your turn you lay one card on the table: a five, which opens its suit, or the card next to either end of a suit already open. Below the five a suit runs down to the ace; above it, up to the seven, then the sota, the caballo and the rey. If you have a card that fits you have to play one. With nothing that fits, you pass.

The first player to lay their last card wins the hand, and scores a point for every card the others still hold. The totals carry from hand to hand; once anybody reaches the target the table chose, the highest total wins the game.

Chinchón

The Spanish rummy: draw, throw, meld sets and runs, and close with little left loose. Two to four players.

Chinchón is played by two, three or four, each for themselves, with the Spanish pack of forty: ace to seven, and the sota, caballo and rey of oros, copas, espadas and bastos.

Each player is dealt seven cards and the next is turned up to start the pile. On your turn you draw one card, from the stock or the top of the pile, and throw one onto the pile; not the card you just took from it.

Cards are melded in sets, three or four of the same number, and runs, three or more of one suit in order: ace to seven, then sota, caballo and rey. Every card counts its number; a figure counts ten.

Instead of throwing, you may close, laying your last card face down: when what is left out of your melds counts 3 or less. Then everybody lays out their melds, the others lay what they can on the closer's melds, and each adds what is left loose to their total. Closing with everything melded takes ten off. Seven cards in a run of one suit, a chinchón, wins the game at once.

When the stock is gone, the pile but for its top card is shuffled into a new one. A player whose total passes the table's limit is out of the game, and the last player in wins it.

Mus

Two pairs, four cards, four lances: grande, chica, pares and juego. Envido, quiero, órdago.

Mus is played two against two, partners facing, with the Spanish pack of forty, and here with eight reyes: a three counts as a rey and a two as an ace. A hand reads from the top: rey, caballo, sota, 7, 6, 5, 4, ace. For the juego a figure or a three counts ten, an ace or a two one, the rest their number.

Each player is dealt four cards. The player after the dealer is mano and speaks first. In turn each says mus, to change cards, or no mus. If all four say mus, everybody changes one to four cards and it goes round again; anybody who says no mus starts the lances.

There are four lances, played in order. Grande: the highest cards win. Chica: the lowest. Pares: a pair; medias, three of a number; duples, two pairs or four of a number, best of all. Juego: 31 or more, 31 the best, then 32, 40, 37, 36, 35, 34 and 33. If nobody has juego the punto is played instead: the most up to 30. At pares and juego only those who have them take part. A tie goes to the player nearer the mano.

At each lance, in turn, a player may pass (paso), envite (envido, two or more) or put an órdago, the whole game. Against a bet the other pair answer: quiero, no quiero, or more. A bet not wanted gives the bettors one, or what was wanted before the last raise, at once. An órdago wanted is decided on the spot, and with it the game.

Then the hands are shown and counted: a lance in paso gives one (grande and chica), a wanted bet its points, and the pares (a pair 1, medias 2, duples 3) and the juego (31 is 3, any other 2) score for each player of the pair that takes the lance; the punto gives one. The first pair to the target wins.

Sueca

Follow suit, take the aces and the sevens, and pass sixty. Four in pairs.

Sueca is played two against two, partners facing, here with the Spanish pack of forty: ace to seven, and the sota, caballo and rey of oros, copas, espadas and bastos.

Every card is dealt, ten each, and the dealer's last card is shown: its suit is trumps, and the card stays in the dealer's hand.

The player after the dealer leads, and play goes round anticlockwise unless the table chose otherwise. Follow the suit led if you can, with any card of it; with none of it, play anything: you are never bound to beat the trick or to trump.

The highest trump takes the trick; with no trump in it, the highest card of the suit led. From highest to lowest a suit runs: ace, 7, rey, sota, caballo, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2.

The cards count: ace 11, seven 10, rey 4, sota 3, caballo 2, the rest nothing: 120 in all, and nothing more for the last trick.

The side with more than 60 takes the hand and one game; with 91 or more, two games; with all 120, four. At 60 each nobody scores. The first side to have the number of games the table chose wins the match.

Butifarra

Choose trumps or say butifarra, double with contro, and take the nines. Four in pairs.

Butifarra is played two against two, partners facing, with the Spanish pack of forty-eight: ace to nine, and the sota, caballo and rey of oros, copas, espadas and bastos.

Every card is dealt, twelve each. The dealer looks at their hand and names trumps; or says butifarra, for no trumps and a hand that counts double; or leaves the choice to their partner, who then has to make it.

Then the other pair may double the hand, contro, the player after the dealer asked first. After a contro the pair that chose may double again, recontro, and after that the others once more, sant Vicenç: two, four and eight times.

The player after the dealer leads, and play goes round anticlockwise unless the table chose otherwise. Follow the suit led, and beat the best card of it if you can; with none of it, play a trump, over any trump already in the trick if you can; with no trump that has to be played, play anything. When your partner is taking the trick you only have to follow suit, with any card of it.

From highest to lowest a suit runs: 9 (the manilla), ace, rey, caballo, sota, 8, 7, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2. The nine counts 5, the ace 4, the rey 3, the caballo 2 and the sota 1, and every trick one more: 72 in a hand.

The pair with more than 36 scores what it took over 36, doubled at butifarra and times the doublings. At 36 each nobody scores. The first pair to the points the table chose wins the game.

Pocha

Say how many tricks you will take, and take exactly that. Three to five, each alone.

Pocha is played by three to five, each alone, with the Spanish pack of forty: ace to seven, and the sota, caballo and rey of oros, copas, espadas and bastos.

The first hand is one card each, the next two, and so on up to as many as the pack allows: thirteen at three, ten at four, eight at five. In a full game the hands then come back down to one. After the deal the next card is turned up and its suit is trumps; when every card is dealt, the dealer's last card is shown instead and stays in the dealer's hand.

Then each player in turn, starting after the dealer, says how many tricks they will take, the dealer last. The dealer may not say the number that would make all the bids add up to the tricks there are, so somebody always misses.

The player after the dealer leads, and play goes round anticlockwise unless the table chose otherwise. Play is bound, as in Tute: follow the suit led and beat the best card of it if you can; with none of it, play a trump, over any trump already in the trick if you can; with no trump that has to be played, play anything. The highest trump takes the trick, or the highest card of the suit led. From highest to lowest a suit runs: ace, three, rey, caballo, sota, 7, 6, 5, 4, 2.

Taking exactly the tricks you bid scores 10, and 5 more for every trick. Missing your bid costs 5 for every trick over or under. When the last hand is played, the most points win.

Ronda

Take your card's rank and the run up from it; score rondas, tringas, darbas and missas. Two, or four in pairs.

Ronda is played one against one, or two against two with partners facing, with the Spanish pack of forty: ace to seven, and the sota, caballo and rey of oros, copas, espadas and bastos.

Each player is dealt four cards, and at the first deal four more are laid face up on the table, no two of a rank. When the hands are empty four more are dealt each; the last deal shares out what is left.

The player after the dealer plays first, and play goes round anticlockwise unless the table chose otherwise. On your turn play a card. If the table holds a card of its rank, your card takes it, and with it the run going up from there: a 3 takes the 3, then the 4 if it is there, then the 5, and so on, the sota coming after the 7. With nothing of its rank on the table, your card is laid down.

Points come as you play. Taking the card the player before you has just laid is a darba: a point. Clearing the table is a missa: a point, but not with the last card of the hand. When the cards are dealt, holding a pair is a ronda, a point, and three of a kind a tringa, five; when both sides hold one, only the higher scores.

When the pack is played out the last cards on the table go to whoever took last, and every card a side has taken over twenty is a point. The points carry from hand to hand, and the game goes to the first side to the target.

How the cards are dealt

Ronda is dealt differently from one town to the next, so the host chooses.

Four on the table: at the first deal four cards go face up on the table, no two of a rank, and each player gets four; the pack does not divide evenly, so the last deal shares out what is left.

None on the table: nothing goes face up, and each player gets four; at two the pack makes five full deals.

Three each: four face up at the first deal, and three cards each every time; every deal is full, at two and at four. With three cards a pair is still a ronda and three of a kind a tringa.

Siete y medio

Get as near seven and a half as you dare, without going over, and beat the bank. Two to six.

Siete y medio is played by two to six with the Spanish pack of forty: ace to seven, and the sota, caballo and rey of oros, copas, espadas and bastos. Each card counts its number, and the sota, caballo and rey half a point each.

One player banks each hand, the dealer, and the bank goes round the table; every player banks the number of times the table chose. Each hand every player plays the bank for a stake of their own.

Everyone is dealt one card face down. Looking at it, each player but the banker stakes 5 to 50 against the bank, in turn from the player after the banker. Then, starting after the banker, each player in turn asks for more cards, dealt face up, or stands. Going over seven and a half loses your stake straight away.

The banker plays last, the same way. Then each player who stood beats the bank with more than the bank holds, and loses with the same or less: ties go to the bank. If the bank goes over, every player still in is paid. Seven and a half exactly is paid double, unless the bank has it too.

When every player has banked, the most chips win.

Julepe

Play the hand or pass; every trick takes a fifth of the pot, and fewer than two is a julepe. Three to six.

Julepe is played by three to six, each alone, with the Spanish pack of forty: ace to seven, and the sota, caballo and rey of oros, copas, espadas and bastos.

Every hand each player puts 5 chips in the pot. Five cards are dealt to each and the next card is turned up: its suit is trumps.

Then, starting after the dealer, each says whether they play the hand or pass; a player who passes throws their cards in. If only one plays, they take the pot without a card played; if nobody plays, the pot waits for the next hand.

Then each who plays, in turn from the first of them, may throw away cards and draw as many from what is left of the pack, while it lasts.

Those who play take the five tricks. The player after the dealer leads, or the next who plays. Play is bound, as in Tute: follow the suit led and beat the best card of it if you can; with none of it, play a trump, over any trump already in the trick if you can; with no trump that has to be played, play anything. From highest to lowest a suit runs: ace, three, rey, caballo, sota, 7, 6, 5, 4, 2.

Each trick takes a fifth of the pot. A player who played and took fewer than two tricks is julepe'd, and puts as much as the pot held into the next one. The deal goes round the table the number of times the host chose; then the most chips win.
